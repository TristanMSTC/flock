01 / Who we are

We are Ban Flock Cameras Nationwide.

We are a group of college students who believe Americans should be able to drive to class, work, a doctor's office, or a friend's house without that trip being photographed, logged, and stored in a searchable database.

We are not against public safety. We are against building a permanent map of where ordinary, innocent people go. That is why we advocate for a nationwide ban on Flock cameras, and for clear federal limits on surveillance technology.

"Privacy Over Policy."

02 / The cameras

What is a Flock camera?

Flock cameras are automated license plate readers (ALPRs) sold by Flock Safety to police departments, homeowners associations, and businesses. They are small, solar powered, and usually mounted on poles at intersections and neighborhood entrances.

Photographs every car

Not just suspects. Every vehicle that passes the camera gets captured, day and night.

Reads the plate

Software converts the license plate into searchable text and flags it against watchlists.

Fingerprints the vehicle

Flock markets a "Vehicle Fingerprint": make, color, type, roof racks, stickers, and damage. Even without a readable plate.

Logs time and place

Each read is time-stamped, geotagged, uploaded to the cloud, and can be shared with other agencies.

Simulation

One camera is a photo. A network is a timeline.

This is a made-up example of how plate reads from different cameras can be stitched together into one person's day.

QUERY plate = "7XKR209"   range = 24h   network = ALL

    INFERRED PROFILE Home address · School · Workplace · Medical visit · Place of worship · Overnight location

    No warrant. No suspicion. Just a person going about their day.

    03 / Why it matters

    Where you go reveals who you are.

    Location data shows where you live, work, worship, get medical care, protest, and sleep. Collected at scale, it is one of the most revealing records that exists about a person.

    Every car passing a camera is recorded, whether or not the driver is suspected of anything
    0 comprehensive federal laws governing how ALPR data is collected, kept, or shared
    1000s of communities across the U.S. now use Flock cameras, many sharing data across a network
    A

    Mass collection without suspicion

    Traditional policing starts with a reason to follow someone. ALPR networks record everyone first and ask questions later. The overwhelming majority of people in these databases have done nothing wrong.

    B

    Sharing and misuse

    Once data exists, it gets used in ways no one voted for. Journalists have reported cases of officers running searches for personal reasons and local data being searched for out-of-state investigations.

    C

    Almost no oversight

    Rules on retention, access, and sharing come from a patchwork of local contracts and company policy. Your rights should not depend on which town you drive through.

    Why students should care

    Our generation already lives with apps that track every click. Flock brings that same tracking into the physical world, onto the roads we use to get to class, work, and home. We will live with these systems longer than anyone. The rules get written now.

    04 / Our case

    Four reasons for a nationwide ban.

    01

    Americans have a reasonable expectation of privacy.

    In Carpenter v. United States (2018), the Supreme Court held that police generally need a warrant to obtain long-term cellphone location records, because tracking movement over time reveals the "privacies of life." A camera network that tracks your car across a city raises the same concern. Being in public is not consent to being followed everywhere.

    02

    Surveillance data will be misused.

    Any database big enough to be useful is big enough to be abused: by an officer with a grudge, a hacker, a data broker, or a future government with different priorities. The safest data is data that was never collected.

    03

    There are no clear limits.

    Who can search this data, for what reason, and for how long? The answers change from town to town and contract to contract. A federal standard is the only way to give every American the same protection.

    04

    People should live without being logged.

    When people know they are watched, they change what they do. They skip the protest, avoid the clinic, think twice about the mosque or the support group. That chilling effect undermines free speech and free association.

    They say

    "Flock cameras keep communities safe."

    Supporters argue the cameras help police recover stolen vehicles, find missing people, respond to Amber Alerts, and identify suspects in serious crimes.

    We respond

    • Useful is not the same as constitutional. Warrantless home searches would solve crimes too. We still do not allow them.
    • It is a dragnet, not an investigation. To find one car, the system records millions of innocent drivers.
    • There are alternatives. Warrant-based tools, targeted alerts, community policing, and well-funded detective work.
    • Local safeguards keep failing. A nationwide ban is the one rule that cannot be quietly worked around.

    05 / Mission

    Our mission is to win a nationwide ban on Flock cameras to protect Americans' privacy, preserve individual freedom, and prevent the misuse of surveillance technology.
    1

    Educate

    Make sure the public knows these cameras exist and what happens to their data.

    2

    Investigate

    Push Congress to hold hearings on automated license plate surveillance and its misuse.

    3

    Mobilize

    Build public support strong enough that lawmakers must take a position on a ban.

    4

    Future-proof

    Win rules that keep pace with new tech like drones and facial recognition, before the damage is done.

    06 / Strategy

    How we lobby Congress.

    1. Direct contact

      Meet members of Congress and their staff, focusing on the House and Senate Judiciary Committees, and bring constituents with real stories.

    2. Letters and petitions

      Organize letter-writing campaigns and a national petition asking lawmakers to sponsor and support a ban.

    3. Awareness

      Social media, campus discussions, tabling, and teach-ins so more people understand what is being collected.

    4. Research and evidence

      Gather public records, audits, and investigations on collection, retention, sharing, and documented misuse.

    5. Coalitions

      Partner with civil liberties and privacy groups across the political spectrum. Privacy is not a left or right issue.

    08 / FAQ
    
        
    Questions we hear a lot.
    
        
    
          If I have nothing to hide, why should I care?
          
    Privacy is not about hiding wrongdoing. You close the bathroom door and you do not post your bank password. Everyone has things that are private but legal: medical visits, relationships, religion, politics.
    
        
    
        
    
          The street is public. Isn't there no privacy in public?
          
    One person seeing your car is very different from a computer recording every trip you take for weeks and letting thousands of officers search it. The Supreme Court recognized that difference for phone location data in Carpenter v. United States.
    
        
    
        
    
          Why a ban instead of regulation?
          
    Local rules and company policies have been tried and keep getting broken, worked around, or quietly changed in contract renewals. A ban is clear, enforceable, and the same for everyone.
    
        
    
        
    
          Won't crime go up without these cameras?
          
    Police solved crimes long before ALPR networks existed, and targeted, warrant-based tools remain available. We oppose the mass tracking of innocent people, not investigation.
    
        
    
        
    
          Are you against the police?
          
    No. We are against a specific technology that records everyone by default. Strong communities need both public safety and civil liberties.
