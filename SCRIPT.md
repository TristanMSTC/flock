# Ban Flock Cameras Nationwide — Presentation Script

**Slogan:** "Privacy Over Policy."
**Speakers:** Adeen · Andrew · Tristan · Paulo
**Target length:** about 8–10 minutes (roughly 60–75 seconds per slide)

## Slide 1 — Intro
**Speaker: Adeen** · ~60 sec

Good [morning/afternoon], everyone. We are **Ban Flock Cameras Nationwide**, and our slogan is simple: **"Privacy Over Policy."**

Picture your normal day. You drive to campus, stop for coffee, go to work, visit a friend, and head home. Now imagine that every one of those stops was photographed, time-stamped, and saved in a database, and that police in other cities, or even other states, could search that database without a warrant. You were never suspected of anything, but your day was recorded anyway.

That is not a hypothetical. That is how automated license plate reader networks like Flock work in thousands of communities across the country right now.

Our group is advocating for a **nationwide ban on Flock cameras**. We are not against public safety. We are against building a permanent, searchable map of where ordinary Americans go. Over the next few minutes we will explain what these cameras are, why this matters, what the other side says, and how we plan to take this to Congress.

*Transition:* "So what exactly is a Flock camera? Andrew is going to walk us through it."

## Slide 2 — What Are Flock Cameras?
**Speaker: Andrew** · ~75 sec

Thanks, Adeen.

Flock cameras are **automated license plate readers**, or ALPRs, made by a company called Flock Safety. They are small, solar-powered cameras usually mounted on poles at intersections, neighborhood entrances, and shopping centers. You have probably driven past dozens without noticing.

Here is what they do:

- **They photograph every passing car,** not just suspicious ones. Every car.
- **They read the license plate** and turn it into searchable text.
- **They record more than the plate.** Flock advertises what it calls a "Vehicle Fingerprint": the make, color, body type, and details like roof racks, bumper stickers, or a dented door. That means a car can be identified even without a readable plate.
- **They log the time and location** and upload it to the cloud.

Police departments, homeowners associations, and private businesses buy these cameras. The key point is that the data does not stay local. Agencies can **share access across a network**, so one department can often search plate reads collected by cameras in many other jurisdictions.

On its own, one photo of your car is not a big deal. But thousands of cameras, all connected, can show **where you go, when you go, and how often.** That is the difference between a camera and a tracking system.

*Transition:* "So why should we care? Tristan."

## Slide 3 — Why Is This an Important Issue?
**Speaker: Tristan** · ~75 sec

Thanks, Andrew.

This matters because **location data tells your whole life story.** Where you drive shows where you live, where you work, what doctor you see, where you worship, who you date, which protest you went to, and when you are not home.

There are three big problems.

**First, mass collection without suspicion.** Traditionally, police need a reason to follow someone. Flock flips that. Everyone is recorded first, and questions get asked later. Ninety-nine percent of the people in these databases have done nothing wrong.

**Second, sharing and misuse.** Once data exists, it gets used in ways nobody voted for. There have been news reports of officers using ALPR searches for personal reasons, like tracking a former partner, and reports of local data being searched for out-of-state investigations and immigration enforcement. Even when rules exist, they are only as strong as the person typing into the search bar.

**Third, almost no oversight.** There is no comprehensive federal law that governs how license plate data is collected, how long it is kept, or who can see it. It is a patchwork of local contracts and company policies.

And this hits **our generation especially hard.** We grew up online. We already know what it feels like to have apps track our every click. Flock brings that same tracking to the physical world, and most people never agreed to it.

*Transition:* "Paulo is going to lay out our main arguments."

## Slide 4 — Our Main Arguments
**Speaker: Paulo** · ~75 sec

Thanks, Tristan. Our case rests on four arguments.

**1. Americans have a reasonable expectation of privacy.** In *Carpenter v. United States* (2018), the Supreme Court ruled that police generally need a warrant to get long-term cellphone location records, because tracking someone's movements over time reveals the "privacies of life." We think the same logic applies to a network of cameras that tracks your car across a city. Being in public does not mean agreeing to be tracked everywhere.

**2. Surveillance data can be misused.** Any database big enough to be useful is big enough to be abused, by an officer with a grudge, a hacker, or a future government with different priorities. The safest data is data that was never collected.

**3. There are no clear limits.** Who can search this data? For what reasons? For how long? Those answers vary from town to town and contract to contract. Rights should not depend on your zip code.

**4. People should be free to live without being logged.** When people know they are being watched, they change their behavior. They may skip a protest, avoid a clinic, or think twice before visiting a mosque or a support group. That "chilling effect" hurts free speech and free association.

*Transition:* "Of course, not everyone agrees with us. Adeen will address the other side."

## Slide 5 — Counterarguments and Responses
**Speaker: Adeen** · ~75 sec

The strongest argument against us is **public safety.** Supporters say Flock cameras help police find stolen cars, locate missing people, respond to Amber Alerts, and identify suspects in serious crimes. We take that seriously. Nobody here wants crime to go up.

Here is our response.

**First, being useful does not make something constitutional.** Searching every home without a warrant would probably solve more crimes too, but we do not allow it, because freedom has value. The question is not "does this help police?" The question is "what are we giving up, and is it worth it?"

**Second, the trade is not targeted.** To find one stolen car, the system records millions of innocent drivers. That is a dragnet, not an investigation.

**Third, there are alternatives.** Police solved car thefts and kidnappings before Flock. Warrant-based investigations, targeted alerts, community policing, and better-funded detective work do not require tracking the entire population.

**Fourth, the safeguards have not held up.** Local policies and "trust us" promises keep getting broken. A nationwide ban is the only rule that cannot be quietly worked around.

*Transition:* "So what are we actually fighting for? Andrew."

## Slide 6 — Our Mission and Goals
**Speaker: Andrew** · ~60 sec

**Our mission** is to win a nationwide ban on Flock cameras to protect Americans' privacy, preserve individual freedom, and prevent the misuse of surveillance technology.

We have four goals.

1. **Educate the public.** Most people have no idea these cameras exist, let alone that their data may be shared. Awareness is step one.
2. **Push Congress to investigate.** We want hearings on automated license plate surveillance: how much data exists, who has access, and how it has been misused.
3. **Build support for a nationwide ban.** We want public pressure strong enough that lawmakers have to take a position.
4. **Protect rights as technology changes.** Flock is today's problem. Tomorrow it might be drones or facial recognition. We want rules that keep up with technology, not rules written after the damage is done.

*Transition:* "Here is how we would actually make that happen. Tristan."

## Slide 7 — How We Would Lobby Congress
**Speaker: Tristan** · ~75 sec

An interest group is only as strong as its plan. Here is ours.

**1. Direct contact.** We would meet with members of Congress and their staff, especially members of the Judiciary Committees, which handle privacy and Fourth Amendment issues. We would bring constituents with real stories, because lawmakers listen to the people who vote for them.

**2. Letters and petitions.** We would organize letter-writing campaigns and a national petition asking lawmakers to sponsor and support a ban. A pile of personal letters from constituents gets a lot more attention than one email.

**3. Awareness campaigns.** Social media, campus events, and tabling. Our website lets anyone send a pre-written letter to their representative in under two minutes.

**4. Research and evidence.** We would collect public records, news investigations, and audits on how data is collected, how long it is kept, who it is shared with, and documented cases of misuse. Facts persuade policymakers better than fear.

**5. Coalitions.** We would partner with established civil liberties and privacy organizations, and with groups across the political spectrum. Privacy is not a left or right issue. Both sides have reasons to distrust unchecked government surveillance, and a bipartisan coalition is how bills actually pass.

## Slide 8 — Conclusion
**Speaker: Paulo** · ~60 sec

To wrap up:

Flock cameras record millions of innocent drivers, store their movements, and make that data searchable across a growing network, with almost no national rules about how it is used.

We believe **public safety and personal freedom both matter.** But when a technology treats every single American as a suspect, the balance is off.

This affects all of us, especially students and young adults who will live with these systems longer than anyone. The choices made now decide what kind of country we inherit.

So we are asking you to do three things: **learn** how these cameras work, **talk** about it with the people you know, and **contact** your representatives and tell them to support a nationwide ban.

Because at the end of the day, your daily life should not be a data point.

**Privacy Over Policy.** Thank you. We are happy to take questions.

## Likely Q&A (have these ready)

**"If you have nothing to hide, why worry?"**
Privacy is not about hiding wrongdoing. You close the bathroom door, and you do not post your bank password. Everyone has things that are private but not illegal, like medical visits, relationships, and religion.

**"Why ban it instead of just regulating it?"**
Regulations have been tried locally and keep getting broken or worked around. A ban is clear, enforceable, and cannot be quietly changed in a contract renewal.

**"Isn't the street public? There's no privacy in public."**
One person seeing your car is different from a computer recording every trip you take for weeks. The Supreme Court made that distinction in *Carpenter* for phone location data.

**"Won't crime go up?"**
Police investigated crimes long before ALPR networks, and targeted, warrant-based tools still exist. We are against mass tracking of innocent people, not investigation.
